  1. For one man, the causal effect of smoking on TB death is the difference between: (a) His risk and the average risk of non-smokers; (b) His risk before and after he started smoking; (c) His outcome if he smokes and if he never smokes; (d) TB risk in smokers and non-smokers in his village.
  2. Selvam smoked and died of TB. Had he never smoked, he would still have died of TB. For Selvam, smoking: (a) Made no difference to his death; (b) Caused his death from TB; (c) Protected him from an earlier death; (d) Doubled his risk of dying of TB.
  3. If all 100 men smoked, 30 would die of TB. If none smoked, 10 would. The causal risk ratio is: (a) 0.20; (b) 0.33; (c) 2.00; (d) 3.00.
  4. Smokers and non-smokers are exchangeable when: (a) Both groups contain the same number of men; (b) Swapping exposures would swap their risks; (c) Both groups were sampled from the same town; (d) Smoking was recorded the same way in both.
  5. Crude RR for smoking and TB death is 2.1. It is 1.0 among drinkers and 1.0 among non-drinkers. Most likely: (a) Alcohol confounds; smoking has no effect; (b) Alcohol is a mediator of smoking's effect; (c) Smoking's effect differs by alcohol use; (d) The stratified figures are less reliable.
  6. Why does randomising who smokes give an unbiased risk ratio? (a) It makes the trial larger than a cohort; (b) It measures smoking more accurately; (c) It removes chance from the comparison; (d) It balances all other causes on average.

BeforeQuestions 7 to 12

  1. Which of these is a back-door path from smoking to TB death? (a) Smoking → Undernutrition → TB death; (b) Smoking → Hospital ← TB death; (c) Smoking ← Alcohol → TB death; (d) Smoking → Cough → TB diagnosis.
  2. Smoking lowers birth weight, which raises infant deaths. For the total effect of smoking, adjusting for birth weight: (a) Removes confounding by birth weight; (b) Removes part of the effect you want; (c) Improves precision with no other change; (d) Is required by most journals.
  3. Smoking and TB are unrelated in a town (OR 1.0). Both raise the chance of admission. Among admitted patients the OR is 0.45. This is: (a) Confounding by age; (b) A true protective effect; (c) Recall bias by patients; (d) Selection on a collider.
  4. 40% of men drink. TB risks: drinkers 0.6 if smokers, 0.3 if not; non-drinkers 0.2 and 0.1. The standardised risk ratio is: (a) 1.5; (b) 3.0; (c) 2.0; (d) 4.0.
  5. For a total effect, which kind of variable should you never adjust for? (a) One caused by the exposure; (b) One that causes the outcome; (c) A common cause of both; (d) One measured at baseline.
  6. One model, built for cooking fuel, also gives an OR of 1.32 for mother's education. That 1.32 is: (a) The total effect of mother's education; (b) Not a total effect: fuel is a mediator; (c) Unbiased, because it is adjusted; (d) Smaller than the true effect, always.
