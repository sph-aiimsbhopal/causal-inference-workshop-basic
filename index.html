Causal Inference in Epidemiology

A self-paced course for beginners: counterfactuals, causal diagrams, bias, and choosing what to adjust for. Anchored to Rothman's Modern Epidemiology, 4th edition.

Who this is for

Postgraduate students in Community Medicine who have met relative risks and 2 × 2 tables, and nothing more. Every idea is explained in plain words, worked with numbers, and practised on a published study. Go through it in order, at your own pace, or follow the workshop path.

The modules

0Why causal questions are hard
Two studies of the same hormones gave opposite answers about heart disease. What went wrong?1Two possible futures
What we mean when we say smoking causes TB death: a comparison of two futures for the same people.1aWhy a coin toss works
A trial recovers an average of futures nobody can see. How, and what it still cannot fix.1bWhose effect? The estimand
One programme can have several correct effects, depending on whom you average over.1cEffects that differ
The same treatment can help some people far more than others, and whether it does depends on the scale.2Association is not causation
What happens when we compare the men who did smoke with the men who did not, and why randomisation fixes it.3A well-defined exposure
Positivity and consistency in plain words: can everyone be exposed, and what exactly is 'the exposure'?4Causes come in teams
Rothman's sufficient-component cause model: why one disease has many causes, and why their shares add up to more than 100%.5Drawing a causal diagram
A causal diagram is a picture of your assumptions. Learn the four rules and how to read paths.6The fork: confounding
A common cause can make two things look linked when neither causes the other. Learn to spot it and remove it.7The chain: mediators
When the exposure works through a middle variable, adjusting for that variable removes the effect you wanted.8The collider: selection bias
Two causes of the same thing become linked when you look only at people who have that thing.9Measurement error in a diagram
We analyse the exposure as recorded, not as it was. When does that bias the answer, and which way?10Choosing what to adjust for
Put the three building blocks together: a four-step recipe for picking an adjustment set from a diagram.11One model, one question
Why the other coefficients in your regression table are not effects: the Table 2 fallacy.12When a confounder was never measured
Negative controls and a first bias analysis: how strong would the missing confounder need to be?13Ask it like a trial
Target trial emulation and time zero: how the hormone therapy puzzle was finally solved.14Capstone: cooking fuel and childhood ARI
Use everything so far on a published NFHS-4 analysis: draw the diagram, find the adjustment set, test the authors' model.15Your own causal question
Write the trial you wish you could run, draw its diagram, and choose the adjustment set before you see the data.
