Module 4. Rothman's sufficient-component cause model: why one disease has many causes, and why their shares add up to more than 100%.

One disease, many causes, working together

TB needs M. tuberculosis. But most infected people never fall ill. Something else has to be present too.

Rothman's idea: a disease happens when a complete set of causes comes together. He drew each set as a pie.

Rothman KJ. Causes. Am J Epidemiol 1976;104:587-92. Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 3.

The sufficient-component cause model is Rothman's own, and it complements the potential-outcome view of Module 1.

A pie is a sufficient cause

Each slice is a component cause.

When every slice is present, the pie is complete and disease follows. The whole pie is a sufficient cause.

Remove any one slice and this pie cannot complete. That is prevention.

The pie is a teaching picture, not biology to the letter. Its slices are the factors that must all be present for this route to disease.

M. tb is in every pie: a necessary cause. No pie without it.

Made-up shares, to show the arithmetic.

Real TB has many more pies. Three are enough to make the next point.

How many deaths could removing each cause prevent?

RemovePies brokenDeaths prevented
SmokingA and C50 + 20 = 70
UndernutritionA50
HIVB and C30 + 20 = 50
DiabetesB30

70 + 50 + 50 + 30 = 200 deaths "caused", out of 100. Attributable fractions do not have to add up to 100%.

Each death needed several causes, so each death is attributable to each of them. This is why "smoking causes half of TB deaths" and "undernutrition causes half" can both be true.

A cause's strength depends on its partners

Suppose smoking causes TB death only together with a partner cause U, say undernutrition. Background risk 1%; 10% of smokers with U die of TB.

This explains why smoking can look more dangerous for TB in India than in a well-fed population: its partner slices are common here.

Pies and the four kinds of men

  • Smoking-caused men are those whose pie needed smoking to complete.
  • Doomed men complete a pie without smoking, for example through HIV and diabetes.
  • Immune men never complete any pie, smoking or not.

Of 100 cases of low birth weight: 40 arose from pie (maternal anaemia + short birth interval), 35 from (anaemia + smoking), 25 from (hypertension + smoking).

How many cases could treating anaemia prevent? Stopping smoking? Do the shares add to 100?

Anaemia: 40 + 35 = 75. Smoking: 35 + 25 = 60. Hypertension 25, birth interval 40. Together 200: no, they need not add to 100.

What to carry forward

A disease follows a complete set of component causes. Removing any one prevents every case that needed it.

Attributable fractions overlap and can sum to far more than 100%.

A cause looks strong where its partner causes are common. Effects do not transfer automatically between populations.
