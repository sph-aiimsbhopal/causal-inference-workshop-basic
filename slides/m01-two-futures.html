Module 1. What we mean when we say smoking causes TB death: a comparison of two futures for the same people.

"Smoking causes TB deaths." What does that claim?

Reading 1

Smokers die of TB more often than non-smokers.

A statement about two different groups of men.

Reading 2

If these same men had never smoked, fewer of them would have died of TB.

A statement about the same men under two conditions.

Reading 2 is the causal claim. This module makes it precise, then puts numbers on it.

Ask the room which reading a TB programme officer needs. Reading 2: she wants to know what changes if smoking changes.

Three questions we often mix up

Descriptive

How many smokers died of TB?

One group, counted.

Associational

Do smokers die of TB more often than never smokers?

Tamil Nadu: rate ratio 4.5

Causal

If these same men had never smoked, how many TB deaths would not have happened?

Not yet answered: ?

"If these same men had…" is where causal reasoning begins.

One man, two possible futures

Murugan is 30. Picture two versions of his life from today.

Future A: he smokes

At 58 he dies of TB.

Future B: he never smokes

At 58 he is alive.

For Murugan, smoking caused his TB death: the two futures differ.

Murugan and the other men in this module are illustrative.

Go slowly; this is the foundation of everything else. The two futures are called potential outcomes. Both exist as possibilities before he makes the choice.

Giving the two futures a name

In wordsWritten asFor Murugan
His outcome if he smokesYa=1dies
His outcome if he never smokesYa=0lives

Y is the outcome, TB death. a is the exposure: 1 means smokes, 0 means never smokes.

These are called potential outcomes. The causal effect for one man is the comparison of his two potential outcomes.

Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 3. Hernán and Robins, What If, chapter 1.

Do not dwell on the notation. Students will meet it in Rothman and in Hernán's book; this slide just makes it familiar.

Selvam smoked and died of TB, yet smoking did not cause his death.

Selvam is the key teaching case. "He smoked and died" is not evidence that smoking killed him, because he would have died anyway.

Each man lives only one of his two futures. The other one is counterfactual: contrary to fact.

So we can never measure the effect for one person. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference.

This is why individual effects are out of reach and why we turn to groups next. Ask: from the visible cells alone, can you tell Murugan from Selvam? No.

Ten men, both columns visible. This never happens in real data; it is a thought experiment that lets us define the effect we want to estimate.

Have them count from the previous slide in the workbook before revealing.

Turn the counts into a causal effect

Risk if all smoked = 6 / 10 = 0.6

Risk if none smoked = 2 / 10 = 0.2

Causal risk difference = 0.6 − 0.2 = 0.4, that is 40 extra TB deaths per 100 men

Causal risk ratio = 0.6 / 0.2 = 3.0, three times the risk

Both compare the same ten men under two conditions. That is what makes them causal.

Risk difference and risk ratio should be familiar from 2 by 2 tables. The new idea is only what is being compared: the same group, twice.

Your turn

In a group of 100 men, if all of them smoked, 25 would die of TB within 30 years. If none of them smoked, 5 would.

Calculate the causal risk difference and the causal risk ratio. Write them in your workbook.

Risk difference = 0.25 − 0.05 = 0.20, 20 extra deaths per 100 men

Risk ratio = 0.25 / 0.05 = 5.0

Give a minute. Reveal the answer only after most have written something. These numbers are invented for practice.

What to carry forward

A causal effect compares the same people under two conditions.

For any one person we see only one of the two, so we work with groups.
