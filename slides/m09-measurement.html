Module 9. We analyse the exposure as recorded, not as it was. When does that bias the answer, and which way?

We never analyse the exposure itself

In Tamil Nadu, smoking was not observed. It was reported, by a family member, often about a man who had died.

What enters the analysis is the box "smoking as reported", not true smoking.

Measurement error drawn as a diagram: the recorded variable is a child of the true one.

Two words to describe the errors

Sensitivity

Of men who truly smoked, the share recorded as smokers.

Specificity

Of men who truly never smoked, the share recorded as never smokers.

You know these from screening tests. Here the "test" is the question about smoking.

Students already know sensitivity and specificity from diagnostic tests; reuse that.

Errors the same for everyone: non-differential

A cohort: 1,000 truly exposed (10% fall ill), 1,000 truly unexposed (5% fall ill). True risk ratio 2.0.

Sensitivity 80% and specificity 90%, the same whether or not a person falls ill.

Recorded asCasesPeopleRisk
Exposed80 + 5 = 85800 + 100 = 9009.4%
Unexposed20 + 45 = 65200 + 900 = 1,1005.9%

Recorded risk ratio = 9.4 / 5.9 = 1.6, closer to 1 than the truth.

Made-up numbers.

Exposed recorded: 80% of the 1,000 exposed (800, with 80 cases) plus 10% of the unexposed wrongly recorded as exposed (100, with 5 cases). The two groups get mixed, so they look more alike.

Non-differential error usually pulls towards 1

With a yes or no exposure and errors that do not depend on the outcome, the recorded effect is usually weaker than the true one.

"Usually", not always: chance, more than two exposure levels, or errors linked to other errors can break the rule.

Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 13.

A common mistake is to say non-differential error "can only bias towards the null" and so a positive finding is safe. Expected direction, not guaranteed direction.

Errors that depend on the outcome: differential

Reporting has errors of its own.

If the outcome changes the error, for instance a widow recalls heavy smoking in a husband who died coughing, the error is differential.

Its bias can go in either direction.

An arrow from outcome to the measurement error is the diagram of differential misclassification.

Recall bias, in numbers

A case-control study: 200 cases, 100 truly exposed; 200 controls, 50 truly exposed. True odds ratio 3.0.

Cases search their memory harder: 95% of exposed cases report it, but only 80% of exposed controls.

ReportedExposedNot exposed
Cases95105
Controls40160

Odds ratio = (95 × 160) / (105 × 40) = 3.6, further from 1 than the truth.

Made-up numbers.

Differential error here inflates the estimate. Reverse the difference in recall and it would shrink it.

Your turn

In NFHS-4, mothers report whether the child had fast or difficult breathing in the last two weeks. Educated mothers may notice it more.

Draw it. Is the error in ARI differential with respect to mother's education? Which way might it bias the education odds ratio?

Education → reporting error → reported ARI. The error depends on the exposure, so it is differential. It would push the education OR up, which may help explain the "surprising" 1.32.

What to carry forward

Draw the recorded variable as its own box. Ask who recorded it, when, and whether they knew the outcome.

Non-differential error in a yes or no exposure usually weakens the estimate: 2.0 became 1.6.

Differential error can go either way: 3.0 became 3.6.
