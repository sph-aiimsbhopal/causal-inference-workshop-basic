Module 0. Two studies of the same hormones gave opposite answers about heart disease. What went wrong?

A cohort study of 59,337 nurses

From 1976, US nurses reported every two years whether they took hormones after menopause. Researchers followed them for heart attacks.

Women who took oestrogen plus progestin, compared with women who never took hormones:

RR 0.39

Grodstein F et al. N Engl J Med 1996;335:453-61.

Keep it concrete. A cohort: women chose for themselves whether to take hormones; the researchers only watched. Reveal the number last.

What does RR 0.39 mean?

RR, the relative risk, is the risk in one group divided by the risk in the other.

Suppose 10 in 1,000 non-users had a heart attack.

Users then had 0.39 × 10 = 3.9 in 1,000.

That is 61% lower risk. Hormones looked strongly protective.

The 10 in 1,000 is only to make the arithmetic visible; the ratio is what the study reported.

Most students know RR loosely. Make them say it as a sentence: users had 39 percent of the non-users' risk. The illustrative baseline is ours; say so.

Doctors acted on it

By the 1990s, hormone therapy was widely prescribed to prevent heart disease, partly on evidence like this.

Then a randomised trial asked the same question.

One beat of history. The point: an observational result changed practice for millions of women.

A randomised trial of 16,608 women

The Women's Health Initiative. A computer decided, like a coin toss, who got oestrogen plus progestin and who got a placebo.

Women given hormones, compared with women given placebo:

HR 1.29

HR, the hazard ratio, reads like a relative risk: 29% more heart disease, not less. The trial was stopped early, at 5.2 years.

Writing Group for the WHI Investigators. JAMA 2002;288:321-33. 95% CI 1.02 to 1.63.

A hazard ratio is a ratio of rates over time; for this course, read it like a relative risk. Stress who decided: the computer, not the woman.
