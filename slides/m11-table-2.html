Module 11. Why the other coefficients in your regression table are not effects: the Table 2 fallacy.

One regression, many odds ratios

Mondal and Paul's model gave an odds ratio for every variable in it.

VariableAdjusted OR
Biomass fuel1.10
No separate kitchen1.22
Household smoking1.06
Mother's education, primary vs none1.32

It is tempting to read each one as "the effect of" that variable. This module shows why only one of them can be.

Mondal D, Paul P. PLoS ONE 2020;15(8):e0237611.

Most published tables look like this. Ask the room: does education really raise ARI by a third?

A model is built for one exposure

The covariates were chosen to block the back-door paths from fuel to ARI.

But from education's point of view, fuel lies on a causal path: Education → Fuel → ARI. Fuel is a mediator of education.

The same model that is right, or nearly right, for fuel is wrong for education: it adjusts for education's mediators.

A worked example: education acting only through fuel

Suppose education's only effect on ARI is through fuel. Made-up numbers.

Biomass usersARI risk
Mothers with no schooling60%0.6 × 15% + 0.4 × 10% = 13%
Mothers with schooling20%0.2 × 15% + 0.8 × 10% = 11%

ARI risk: 15% in biomass homes, 10% in clean-fuel homes, whatever the mother's schooling.

Total effect of schooling: 11 / 13 = 0.85, protective.

Adjusted for fuel: within each fuel group, 15% against 15% and 10% against 10%. Ratio 1.0. The protection has vanished.

Adjusting for fuel answers a different question: the effect of education not through fuel. Here that is zero by construction.

Add one more real-world twist: reporting

Now suppose educated mothers notice and report fast breathing 20% more often, for the same true illness.

Fuel-adjusted ratio: 1.0 × 1.2 = 1.2. Education now looks harmful.

Total effect: 0.85 × 1.2 = 1.02. Nearly no effect.

A protective cause, adjusted for its mediator and measured through mothers' reports, looks harmful. The paper's 1.32 may be built the same way.

We cannot say this is what happened in NFHS-4; the point is that the coefficient has no single causal reading.

The Table 2 fallacy

Reading every coefficient in a multivariable table as a causal effect.

  • The adjustment set is right, at best, for one exposure.
  • For each covariate, the same model may adjust for its mediators and miss its confounders.
  • So covariate coefficients are usually neither total effects nor direct effects.

Westreich D, Greenland S. Am J Epidemiol 2013;177:292-8. The name comes from the second table of most papers.

Many journals now ask authors not to present covariate coefficients, or to label them clearly as not interpretable.

What to do instead

  1. One causal question at a time. One diagram, one adjustment set, one model.
  2. Report the exposure's estimate. Leave the covariates out of the results table, or say plainly they are not effects.
  3. If a second exposure matters, fit a second model with its own adjustment set.
For a thesis with several exposures, this means several models. That is normal, not excessive.

Your turn

A model for smoking and TB death adjusts for age, education and alcohol use. Alcohol use makes men more likely to smoke.

Is the alcohol odds ratio from this model the total effect of alcohol on TB death? Why?

No. Alcohol → Smoking → TB death: smoking is a mediator of alcohol, and the model adjusts for it. Part of alcohol's effect is removed.

Same structure as the education example, in a different study.

What to carry forward

A model is built for one exposure. Its other coefficients are not effects.

A covariate adjusted for its own mediators can even flip direction: 0.85 became 1.2.
