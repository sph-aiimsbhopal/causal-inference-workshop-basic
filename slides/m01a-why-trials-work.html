Module 1a. A trial recovers an average of futures nobody can see. How, and what it still cannot fix.

Everyone carries two hidden cards

From Module 1: each person has two futures. Imagine them written on two cards nobody can read.

Card 1

What happens if treated: Y(1)

Card 2

What happens if not treated: Y(0)

We can only ever turn over one card per person. A trial needs a way round that.

Adapted from Dr Ankur Joshi's hidden-card analogy.

The effect we want: everyone's cards, averaged

1,000 people. Made-up numbers.

10%
would die if all were treated (100 of 1,000)
20%
would die if none were treated (200 of 1,000)

Average effect = 10% − 20% = −10 percentage points: 10 fewer deaths per 100 people treated.

Its name is the average treatment effect, ATE. It is a property of the whole group, not of any one person.

In notation: ATE = E[Y(1)] − E[Y(0)]. For a yes or no outcome, the average is the proportion.

A coin toss lets one half stand in for everyone

Toss a coin for each of the 1,000. Heads: treated. Tails: not.

  • The treated half is a random sample of everyone. Their "treated" cards average about 10%.
  • The untreated half is a random sample too. Their "untreated" cards average about 20%.
ArmDeathsRisk
Treated50 / 50010%
Not treated100 / 50020%

Estimated effect: 10% − 20% = −10 points. The trial recovers the average of cards nobody could read.

This is exchangeability, from Module 2, bought by the coin.

What we want and what we calculate

What we want

The average effect in everyone

Unobservable: needs both cards

What we calculate

Risk in treated arm minus risk in untreated arm

Observable, from the trial

A trial finds 8% deaths with treatment and 12% without. State the effect in words.

−4 points: on average 4 fewer deaths per 100 treated. Not "every patient's risk falls by 4 points".

What the coin balances

On average, the two arms are alike in:

Age, sex, blood pressure

Things we measured

Diet, stress, genes

Things we did not measure

The hidden cards themselves

Each person's two futures

"On average" matters. In a trial of 40 people, the arms can still differ by chance. Bigger trials make large imbalances rarer, not impossible.

Balance is in expectation over repeated randomisations, not guaranteed in any single trial.

A trap: does the coin make treatment unrelated to outcome?

No. The coin makes treatment unrelated to the hidden cards, the futures each person would have.

If the drug works, the treated arm has fewer deaths: 10% against 20%. Treatment and observed outcome should be associated. That association is the effect.

A common misreading of "independence" in trials.

What the coin does not fix

People lost to follow-up

People who stop the drug

Controls who start it

Outcomes measured badly

Too few people

A vague intervention

The coin protects who gets which arm. Everything after that needs its own care.

Analyse as randomised

1,000 people randomised. Then real life happens:

  • 100 of the 500 given the drug stop taking it.
  • 20 of the 500 controls get the drug elsewhere.

Compare all 500 against all 500, as the coin assigned them. This is intention to treat.

Moving people between arms by what they did later breaks what the coin gave you. People who stop a drug are often sicker.

Adapted from Dr Ankur Joshi's lesson.

Intention to treat answers a real question: what happens if we offer this drug?

The WHI result in Module 0, HR 1.29, was an intention-to-treat estimate. Per-protocol effects need the methods of Module 13.

Stopping the drug dilutes the effect

The drug lowers death from 20% to 10% if taken. Half the treated arm stops it and has the control risk. Made-up numbers.

Treated arm: 0.5 × 10% + 0.5 × 20% = 15%

Control arm: 20%

Intention to treat: 15% − 20% = −5 points. The effect of taking it: −10 points.

This assumes those who stop have the control risk.

Intention to treat is not wrong; it answers "offer the drug", not "take the drug".

Your turn

A drug lowers death from 30% to 15% if taken. 40% of the treated arm stop it and have the control risk.

What will the intention-to-treat difference be?

Treated arm: 0.6 × 15% + 0.4 × 30% = 21%. Control 30%. Difference −9 points, against −15 if everyone took it.

Check: 9 + 12 = 21.
