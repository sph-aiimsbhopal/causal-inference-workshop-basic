A causal question is a question about a change

Association

Do smokers die of TB more often than non-smokers?

Compares two different groups of people, as they are.

Effect

How many TB deaths would we prevent if these men had never smoked?

Compares the same people under two different conditions.

If you cannot say what you would change, you do not yet have a causal question.

Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapters 2 and 3.

Session 1 begins, about 25 minutes of concepts. The single most useful habit: phrase the causal question as an intervention. "Is X associated with Y" describes the data. "What would happen to Y if we changed X" is what a programme manager or clinician actually needs. Ask: for the hormone question, what is the change? Starting oestrogen plus progestin at menopause, versus not starting. Keep that in mind; it matters later.

We write them as Ya=1 and Ya=0, the potential outcomes. The causal effect for one person is the difference between them.

We only ever see one of the two. The other is counterfactual: contrary to fact.

Use the names to make it concrete; these men are illustrative. Each man carries two potential outcomes: what would happen if he smoked, what would happen if he did not. The individual causal effect is the comparison of the two. Selvam dies either way, so smoking has no effect on him even though he smoked and died. That is why "he smoked and died of TB" is not proof of anything. Then the catch, often called the fundamental problem of causal inference: for any real man we see only one column. Half of every row is missing.

So we compare groups, and hope they are alike

Causal risk ratio=risk if everyone smokedrisk if no one smoked
Associational risk ratio=risk in those who did smokerisk in those who did not

The two are equal when smokers and non-smokers are exchangeable: had they swapped, each group would have had the other's risk.

Population level. The causal risk ratio compares the whole population with itself under two conditions. The associational ratio compares two different subsets. They coincide only under exchangeability. Ask: would non-smokers have had the smokers' risk if they had smoked? Probably not. Smokers in Tamil Nadu drink more, are poorer, perhaps more undernourished. That difference is what we call confounding. Randomisation gives exchangeability on average, by design. Observational studies have to argue for it, and that argument is the rest of today.

Three conditions before an association can be read as an effect

Exchangeability

No unblocked common causes of exposure and outcome. Ideally, within levels of what you adjust for.

Positivity

Everyone could have been exposed or unexposed. No group where nobody smokes, or everybody does.

Consistency

The exposure is well defined. Bidi or cigarette? Ten a day or one? "Smoking" is many interventions.

"Obesity causes diabetes": by which change in weight, diet, genes, activity? A vague exposure gives a vague answer.

Three identifiability conditions, in plain words. Exchangeability we just met. Positivity: if no one in some subgroup is ever exposed, the data cannot tell you what exposure would do there. Consistency: the exposure must correspond to a reasonably well-defined intervention. Gajalakshmi found TB death risk ratios of 6.3 for urban bidi-only smokers; cigarettes and bidis are different exposures. Obesity is the classic vague exposure: losing weight by exercise, by bariatric surgery or by tuberculosis are not the same intervention. Hernán's paper "Does obesity shorten life?" is a good read on this.

Rothman's pies: a disease needs a complete set of causes

Each pie is one sufficient cause. Each slice is a component cause. M. tuberculosis is in every pie: a necessary cause.

Remove any one slice and that pie cannot complete. That is prevention.

Fractions of disease due to each component can add up to far more than 100%.

"Smoking causes half of male TB deaths in India" and "undernutrition causes half" can both be true.

Illustrative pies. Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 3. Gajalakshmi V et al. Lancet 2003;362:507-15.

The sufficient-component cause model is Rothman's own. A disease happens when one complete pie is assembled. Components act together; that is interaction in the biological sense. Two consequences students find surprising. First, blocking any one component prevents every case that needed that pie, so the attributable fractions overlap and can sum to well over 100 percent. Second, the strength of a cause depends on how common its partner components are: smoking looks more dangerous for TB in India than in Sweden because the other slices, infection and undernutrition, are common here. Link back: Murugan was a man whose pie needed smoking to complete.

Exercise 1 debrief · What the paper tells you, if you look

A clue in the paper itself

Smokers also had 3.3 times the death rate from alcoholism or cirrhosis. The authors put this down to heavy drinkers tending to smoke. Alcohol was not adjusted for.

Who reported smoking?

For dead men, the family. For controls, the family too. Was reporting as accurate for a man who died coughing?

Ever smokers

Ex-smokers were counted with smokers, so men who quit because they fell ill do not flip the result.

Dose and type

Risk rose with amount smoked, and was high for bidis too (urban bidi-only 6.3).

An outcome smoking should barely cause, but alcohol does, came out raised. That is a warning about confounding, written in the paper's own table.

Take two pairs' answers to question 1 and the target trial. Then show this. The cirrhosis ratio of 3.3 works as an informal negative control outcome: smoking itself is not a major cause of alcoholic cirrhosis, so a raised ratio there flags that smokers differ in drinking. That does not destroy the TB finding: dose-response, the bidi result, the self-reported TB survey (smokers three times as likely to report past TB) and biology all support a real effect. It does mean 4.5 may overstate it. This is Rothman's point: causal inference is judgement across evidence, not a single number. Lipsitch's paper on negative controls is on the reading list.

A causal diagram is your assumptions, drawn

  • An arrow means "directly causes", as far as this diagram goes.
  • No loops: nothing causes itself. Time runs one way.
  • Every common cause of two variables in the diagram must be in it, measured or not.
  • A missing arrow is the strongest claim: it says there is no direct effect.

The diagram comes from subject knowledge, not from your data.

Greenland S, Pearl J, Robins JM. Epidemiology 1999;10:37-48. Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 3.

Session 2 begins, about 25 minutes of concepts. Directed acyclic graph: directed because arrows have direction, acyclic because no loops. Stress the last rule: drawing an arrow is cheap, leaving one out is a claim you must defend. Also: you cannot learn the diagram from the dataset you are analysing; it comes from biology, prior studies, how the data were collected. If two experts disagree, draw both diagrams and see whether they lead to different adjustments. Often they do not.

Three building blocks explain almost all bias

Fork

A common cause. Path open. Adjusting closes it.

Chain

A mediator. Path open. Adjusting closes it, and removes part of the effect.

Collider

A common effect. Path closed. Adjusting opens it.

The heart of the session. Go slowly. Fork: alcohol causes both smoking and TB death, so smoking and TB death are associated even with no arrow between them; condition on alcohol and the association from that source disappears. Chain: smoking lowers birth weight, low birth weight raises death; association flows along it; adjusting for birth weight blocks it, which is wrong if you want the total effect. Collider: smoking and birth defects both cause low birth weight; in the population they are unrelated; look only at small babies and they become associated, negatively. Students find the collider counter-intuitive; give the everyday version: among people admitted to a hospital ward, a disease and a risk factor can look linked simply because either gets you admitted.

Reading a path: open or blocked?

A path is any route between exposure and outcome, ignoring arrow direction.

  • A path is blocked if it passes through a collider you have not adjusted for.
  • A path is blocked if it passes through a non-collider you have adjusted for.
  • Otherwise it is open, and association flows along it.
  • Adjusting for a descendant of a collider partly opens it too.

Goal for a total effect

Keep every causal path from exposure to outcome open.

Block every other path.

Bias is association flowing through a non-causal path you left open.

These are the d-separation rules in student language. Do not use the word d-separation unless they ask. Practise once aloud on the fork diagram, then on the collider. Mention that "adjusting" covers many things: putting a variable in a regression, stratifying, matching, restricting the sample, or selecting who enters the study. Selection is adjustment you did not choose.

Confounding is an open back-door path

A back-door path starts with an arrow into the exposure. Smoking ← Alcohol → TB death is one.

A confounder is a variable that can block a back-door path.

"Associated with exposure and outcome, not on the causal pathway" is the old rule. It wrongly admits colliders.

"It changed the estimate by 10%" cannot tell a confounder from a collider or a mediator.

Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 12. Hernán MA et al. Am J Epidemiol 2002;155:176-84.

Show the Gajalakshmi diagram with three open back-door paths: through age, through peer group and alcohol, through alcohol and undernutrition. Education, which they did adjust for, blocks the others. Then hit the two traditional rules. The three-criteria rule can label a collider as a confounder, because a collider is associated with both. Change-in-estimate can't separate confounding from collider bias or mediator adjustment, because all of them change the estimate. Hernán 2002 showed real examples in birth defects epidemiology where the statistical rules led to the wrong adjustment.

Selection bias is adjusting for a collider without meaning to

  • Hospital controls. If both smoking and TB raise the chance of admission, studying admitted patients links them.
  • Loss to follow-up that depends on exposure and on illness.
  • Volunteers who join because they are worried and exposed.
  • Survivors. Women still taking hormones years later are those who did not have an early heart attack on them.

You cannot see this bias in your data. You can only see it in the diagram of how people got into your data.

Hernán MA, Hernández-Díaz S, Robins JM. Epidemiology 2004;15:615-25. Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 14.

Hernán's structural view: selection bias is conditioning on a common effect, here admission. Berkson's original example was exactly hospital-based case-control studies. The survivor bullet sets up the hormone story: if you study women who are currently on hormones, you have selected those who survived the early months of use without an event. Keep it as a teaser.

Measurement error has a structure too

We never analyse true smoking, only smoking as reported.

Non-differential: errors unrelated to the outcome. Usually biases a binary exposure towards the null.

Differential: the outcome changes the reporting. An arrow from TB death to reporting error. Bias in either direction.

Ask of every exposure: who reported it, when, and did they know the outcome?

Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 13.

Keep this to 3 minutes; information bias is not the focus today. The Tamil Nadu study relied on families reporting a dead man's habits. A widow might recall heavy smoking in a man who died of a lung disease. That is the arrow from outcome to error. The same structure is recall bias in case-control studies of birth defects. Chapter 13 and chapter 29 on bias analysis go further.

Debrief · The birth weight paradox is collider bias

Low birth weight is a mediator on smoking → LBW → death, and a collider on smoking → LBW ← defects → death.

Restrict to LBW and both go wrong: part of the effect is cut, and a non-causal path opens.

For decades "adjust for birth weight" was standard practice in perinatal studies.

Do not adjust for a variable caused by the exposure when you want the total effect.

The diagram on the slide has low birth weight adjusted for and the path through birth defects highlighted in red. Walk the path aloud. Then make the general rule. Mention that the same structure explains the "obesity paradox" in heart failure and diabetes, where conditioning on having the disease makes obesity look protective, and that Hernández-Díaz's later work drew birth weight specific mortality curves to show it.

Debrief · Hormones: confounding, and something more

What the diagram shows

An open back-door path through health-seeking behaviour, which was not measured. No adjustment on measured variables can close it.

What Hernán and colleagues did in 2008

Re-analysed the Nurses' Health Study as if it were a trial: eligible women, starting hormones vs not, from the start of use.

0.96hazard ratio, whole follow-up
1.42first 2 years after starting

Much of the gap came from comparing long-term current users with non-users, and from women's age at starting, not only from confounding.

Hernán MA et al. Epidemiology 2008;19:766-79.

This is the twist. The usual textbook explanation is healthy-user confounding, and the diagram supports some of that. But when the cohort data were analysed like the trial, comparing new initiators with non-initiators from the time of starting, the estimates came out similar to the WHI: 0.96 overall, with a raised risk of 1.42 in the first two years. The authors concluded that the discrepancy could be largely explained by differences in time since menopause and length of follow-up. Prevalent users are survivors of the early high-risk period: the selection bias slide. Lesson: draw the diagram, and also define time zero like a trial would. That idea, target trial emulation, is the bridge to further learning.

The back-door rule, in two lines

1 · Block every back-door path

Every path that starts with an arrow into the exposure must be blocked by something in your adjustment set.

2 · Touch nothing downstream

Adjust for nothing caused by the exposure: no mediators, no consequences, no colliders between them.

If both hold and the diagram is right, the adjusted estimate is the total causal effect.

There is often more than one set that works. Prefer one that is measured well.

Pearl's back-door criterion. Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 12.

Session 3, about 15 minutes of concepts. This is Pearl's back-door criterion in plain language. Rule 1 deals with confounding. Rule 2 prevents overadjustment and collider bias through descendants. Emphasise "if the diagram is right": the rule moves the argument from statistics to subject knowledge, which is where it belongs. Multiple valid sets: choose the one with the best-measured variables, since a badly measured confounder only partly blocks its path.

Four things not to adjust for

Mediators

You remove part of the effect you want. Birth weight for smoking.

Colliders

You open a path that was closed. Hospital admission.

Consequences of the outcome

Treatment received, symptoms after diagnosis.

Pure causes of exposure

Not bias, but you lose precision and can amplify other bias.

Causes of the outcome alone are safe and can improve precision. Child age for ARI.

Quick round. Ask the room for an example of each from their own thesis area. The fourth card covers instrument-like variables: a factor that causes the exposure but not the outcome. Adjusting for it does not reduce bias and widens confidence intervals; with unmeasured confounding it can even increase bias. Variables that only cause the outcome are fine to include.

Not sure of the full diagram? A practical rule

  1. Adjust for each variable that causes the exposure, the outcome, or both.
  2. Drop any that you believe causes the exposure only, unless it is needed to block a path.
  3. Add a good proxy for an important unmeasured common cause.
  4. Never include anything caused by the exposure.

Then say clearly, in your methods, which confounders you could not measure.

VanderWeele TJ. Principles of confounder selection. Eur J Epidemiol 2019;34:211-9.

This is VanderWeele's modified disjunctive cause criterion, simplified. It works well when people cannot agree on the full diagram, because you only need to know which variables are causes of exposure or outcome, not every arrow among them. Point out the last line: being explicit about unmeasured confounding is better science than pretending a long covariate list solves it.

The "Table 2 fallacy": reading every coefficient in a multivariable table as an effect.

Westreich D, Greenland S. Am J Epidemiol 2013;177:292-8. Mondal D, Paul P. PLoS ONE 2020;15:e0237611.

Set up the capstone. In a single model, the coefficient for a covariate is adjusted for the exposure and everything else. For mother's education, that means adjusting away its effects through cooking fuel, kitchen, smoking: mediators of education. What remains is not the effect of education. That is one reason an odds ratio above 1 for educated mothers can appear, which the authors found surprising. Another is reporting: educated mothers may notice and report fast breathing more. Do not give all of this away; let groups find it.

Group exercise debrief · What we would ask the authors

Missing common causes

Rural or urban residence and state shape both cooking fuel and ARI. Neither was in the model.

One model, three exposures

Fuel, kitchen and smoking each need their own adjustment set, and their own model.

Who reports the outcome?

Mothers report ARI. If education changes recognition, education affects measured ARI, not only true ARI.

What is "biomass"?

Kerosene, coal, wood and dung were grouped. A cleaner-fuel programme changes one of these, not all.

None of this needs new data to say. It needs a diagram drawn before the analysis.

Collect the one-minute reports, then show this slide as a summary rather than a correction. Be fair to the authors: national survey analyses like this are common and useful for describing burden, and the cross-sectional design limits them too. The point is that the same data, with a diagram, would lead to a different and more defensible model. Ask: would your own thesis survive this review?

Back to the hormones: what you can now say

  • The cohort compared different women; the trial compared the same kind of women under two choices.
  • Some of the gap was confounding through health-seeking, which nobody measured.
  • Some came from studying long-term users, who had already survived the risky early years.
  • Analysed like a trial, the same cohort agreed with the trial.

Observational data did not fail. The question and the comparison were set up badly.

Close the loop with the opening slide. Ask a student to explain the discrepancy to the room in one minute. Then this summary. The emotional point: observational epidemiology is not hopeless; the cohort could answer the question once it was asked the right way.
