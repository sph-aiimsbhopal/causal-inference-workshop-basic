Module 0 · Causal inference for beginners

Why causal questions are hard

Two studies of the same hormones gave opposite answers about heart disease. What went wrong?

Module 0. Two studies of the same hormones gave opposite answers about heart disease. What went wrong?

A cohort study of 59,337 nurses

From 1976, US nurses reported every two years whether they took hormones after menopause. Researchers followed them for heart attacks.

Women who took oestrogen plus progestin, compared with women who never took hormones:

RR 0.39

Grodstein F et al. N Engl J Med 1996;335:453-61.

Keep it concrete. A cohort: women chose for themselves whether to take hormones; the researchers only watched. Reveal the number last.

What does RR 0.39 mean?

RR, the relative risk, is the risk in one group divided by the risk in the other.

Suppose 10 in 1,000 non-users had a heart attack.

Users then had 0.39 × 10 = 3.9 in 1,000.

That is 61% lower risk. Hormones looked strongly protective.

The 10 in 1,000 is only to make the arithmetic visible; the ratio is what the study reported.

Most students know RR loosely. Make them say it as a sentence: users had 39 percent of the non-users' risk. The illustrative baseline is ours; say so.

Doctors acted on it

By the 1990s, hormone therapy was widely prescribed to prevent heart disease, partly on evidence like this.

Then a randomised trial asked the same question.

One beat of history. The point: an observational result changed practice for millions of women.

A randomised trial of 16,608 women

The Women's Health Initiative. A computer decided, like a coin toss, who got oestrogen plus progestin and who got a placebo.

Women given hormones, compared with women given placebo:

HR 1.29

HR, the hazard ratio, reads like a relative risk: 29% more heart disease, not less. The trial was stopped early, at 5.2 years.

Writing Group for the WHI Investigators. JAMA 2002;288:321-33. 95% CI 1.02 to 1.63.

A hazard ratio is a ratio of rates over time; for this course, read it like a relative risk. Stress who decided: the computer, not the woman.

Which one do you believe? Write one sentence in your workbook saying why.

Take three or four answers. Expect: the trial, because randomised; and "healthy women chose hormones". Do not resolve it yet. Module 13 tells the full story.

Module 1 · Causal inference for beginners

Two possible futures

What we mean when we say smoking causes TB death: a comparison of two futures for the same people.

Module 1. What we mean when we say smoking causes TB death: a comparison of two futures for the same people.

"Smoking causes TB deaths." What does that claim?

Reading 1

Smokers die of TB more often than non-smokers.

A statement about two different groups of men.

Reading 2

If these same men had never smoked, fewer of them would have died of TB.

A statement about the same men under two conditions.

Reading 2 is the causal claim. This module makes it precise, then puts numbers on it.

Ask the room which reading a TB programme officer needs. Reading 2: she wants to know what changes if smoking changes.

Three questions we often mix up

Descriptive

How many smokers died of TB?

One group, counted.

Associational

Do smokers die of TB more often than never smokers?

Tamil Nadu: rate ratio 4.5

Causal

If these same men had never smoked, how many TB deaths would not have happened?

Not yet answered: ?

"If these same men had…" is where causal reasoning begins.

One man, two possible futures

Murugan is 30. Picture two versions of his life from today.

Future A: he smokes

At 58 he dies of TB.

Future B: he never smokes

At 58 he is alive.

For Murugan, smoking caused his TB death: the two futures differ.

Murugan and the other men in this module are illustrative.

Go slowly; this is the foundation of everything else. The two futures are called potential outcomes. Both exist as possibilities before he makes the choice.

These are called potential outcomes. The causal effect for one man is the comparison of his two potential outcomes.

Each man lives only one of his two futures. The other one is counterfactual: contrary to fact.

So we can never measure the effect for one person. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference.

This is why individual effects are out of reach and why we turn to groups next. Ask: from the visible cells alone, can you tell Murugan from Selvam? No.

Turn the counts into a causal effect

Risk if all smoked = 6 / 10 = 0.6

Risk if none smoked = 2 / 10 = 0.2

Causal risk difference = 0.6 − 0.2 = 0.4, that is 40 extra TB deaths per 100 men

Causal risk ratio = 0.6 / 0.2 = 3.0, three times the risk

Both compare the same ten men under two conditions. That is what makes them causal.

Risk difference and risk ratio should be familiar from 2 by 2 tables. The new idea is only what is being compared: the same group, twice.

What to carry forward

A causal effect compares the same people under two conditions.

For any one person we see only one of the two, so we work with groups.

Why the study got it wrong

Among the 4 smokers, 3 drink.

Among the 6 non-smokers, 1 drinks.

Drinkers are more likely to die of TB whether or not they smoke.

The smokers would have done worse than the non-smokers even without smoking. Some of the 6.0 is alcohol, not tobacco.

In the Module 1 table, both doomed men, Selvam and Kannan, are drinkers. Drinkers carry more risk of their own.

Smokers' risk without smoking = 1 / 4 = 0.25. Non-smokers' actual risk = 1 / 6 = 0.17.

These should be equal for a fair comparison. They are not.

This is the exchangeability check, done with numbers we normally never have. In a real study we cannot do this calculation; we have to argue for it.

Exchangeability, in one sentence

Two groups are exchangeable if, had they swapped exposures, each would have had the other group's risk.

  • If exchangeable: observed risk ratio = causal risk ratio.
  • If not: the observed ratio mixes the effect with how the groups differ.
  • That mixing is what epidemiologists call confounding.

Greenland S, Robins JM. Int J Epidemiol 1986;15:413-9. Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 12.

One sentence students should be able to say back. Confounding is named here and treated properly in Module 6.

How a trial gets exchangeability: toss a coin

If a coin decides who smokes, drinking cannot influence who ends up in which group.

On average, half of every kind of man lands in each group: half the drinkers, half the doomed, half the immune.

With the 100 men in Lab 1, that gives risks of 0.39 and 0.13 on average: risk ratio 3.0, the true effect.

Any single toss can be off by chance. Bias goes away; chance does not.

This is why the WHI trial is believed over the cohort. Randomisation breaks every link between the exposure and the man's other risks, measured or not.

Now the real study

Gajalakshmi and colleagues: 43,000 men who died in Tamil Nadu and 35,000 living men. Ever smokers had 4.5 times the TB death rate of never smokers (urban men, 25 to 69).

  • Who chose to smoke? Not a coin.
  • Smokers in the same study also had 3.3 times the death rate from alcoholism or cirrhosis.
  • The analysis allowed for age, education and tobacco chewing, but not alcohol.

Are smokers and never smokers exchangeable here? Write what might differ.

Gajalakshmi V, Peto R, Kanaka TS, Jha P. Lancet 2003;362:507-15.

Real numbers. The cirrhosis finding is a clue that smokers drink more. This does not mean smoking has no effect on TB; dose-response and other evidence support a real effect. It means 4.5 may be an overestimate.

Think it through: milk and height

Children who drink more milk are taller.

  1. Is this association or causation?
  2. A child drinks two glasses a day. Which counterfactual would tell us milk's effect?
  3. Milk drinkers come from richer, better fed families with better health care. So what?
Adapted from Dr Ankur Joshi's lesson. Give the room two minutes in pairs before the next slide.

Milk and height: answers

1. Association. Milk and height go together; that milk raises height is not yet shown.

2. The same child, same age, without the milk. If she would be 148 cm instead of 150 cm, milk added 2 cm. If 150 cm anyway, no effect.

3. Not exchangeable. Family wealth drives both milk and growth.

The arrow from milk to height is drawn as the question we want to answer, not as a known fact. Made-up heights.

What to carry forward

An observed comparison is causal only if the groups are exchangeable.

Randomisation buys exchangeability on average. Observational studies have to argue for it.

Module 5 · Causal inference for beginners

Drawing a causal diagram

A causal diagram is a picture of your assumptions. Learn the four rules and how to read paths.

Module 5. A causal diagram is a picture of your assumptions. Learn the four rules and how to read paths.

Why draw a picture?

In Module 2, alcohol made smokers and non-smokers non-exchangeable. Real studies have many such variables.

A causal diagram puts your beliefs about what causes what on paper, so that you, and anyone reading your thesis, can see them and argue with them.

Formally a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Greenland S, Pearl J, Robins JM. Epidemiology 1999;10:37-48.

The diagram is not a statistical model. It is a statement of assumptions, drawn before the analysis.

Boxes and an arrow

Each box is a variable: something that differs between people.

An arrow means: directly causes. Smoking raises the risk of TB death.

Exposure and outcome are shown in dark boxes.

First click adds the arrow and its explanation together. Keep this slide almost trivially simple.

Add what you know about alcohol

Alcohol use is a variable too.

Men who drink are more likely to smoke: an arrow into smoking.

Alcohol raises the risk of TB death: an arrow into TB death.

Each click adds one arrow with its sentence. Ask the room before each click: should there be an arrow here, and which way?

Four rules for drawing

  1. An arrow means a direct cause, pointing from cause to effect.
  2. No loops. Nothing can cause itself. If smoking causes stress and stress causes more smoking, draw smoking at two times.
  3. Every common cause goes in, measured or not. Draw an unmeasured one with a dashed box.
  4. A missing arrow is a claim. It says there is no direct effect at all.
Rule 4 surprises people. Drawing an arrow is cautious; leaving one out is bold. When unsure, draw the arrow.

Where do the arrows come from?

From

Biology, earlier studies, how the data were collected, and the order in which things happen.

Not from

The dataset you are about to analyse: its correlations, p-values or stepwise regression.

If two experts disagree, draw both diagrams. Often they lead to the same adjustment.

Data cannot tell you which way an arrow points or whether a correlation is confounding. That is why the diagram comes first.

A path is any route between two boxes

Follow the lines from smoking to TB death. You may go along an arrow or against it.

Path 1: Smoking → TB death

Path 2: Smoking ← Alcohol → TB death

The highlighted path changes with each click. Students often think paths must follow the arrows; they need not.

Causal paths and the other kind

Causal path

Every arrow points away from the exposure, towards the outcome.

Smoking → TB death

This carries the effect we want.

Non-causal path

At least one arrow points the other way.

Smoking ← Alcohol → TB death

This carries association that is not the effect of smoking.

The observed 4.45 in Lab 1 is the sum of what flows along both paths. We want only the first.

This links back to the numbers: 3.0 along the causal path, plus the extra from alcohol, gives the 4.45 seen.

Alcohol, also shaped by education, raises TB death. It was not adjusted for, so its box is dashed.

Alcohol use is a common cause.

What stratifying did, in the diagram

The path Smoking ← Alcohol → TB death carries association: it is open.

Comparing within levels of alcohol puts alcohol in a box: we adjust for it. The path is now blocked (dashed).

Red means a non-causal path that is open and biasing the result. Dashed means blocked. These conventions are the same in Lab 2.

Giving it names

  • Back-door path: a path that starts with an arrow into the exposure. Smoking ← Alcohol → TB death.
  • Confounding: association that flows through an open back-door path.
  • Confounder: a variable that, adjusted for, blocks a back-door path. Here, alcohol.

Confounding is defined by the diagram, not by a statistical test.

Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 12. Greenland S, Pearl J, Robins JM. Epidemiology 1999;10:37-48.

This is the structural definition. It agrees with the potential-outcome idea from Module 2: an open back-door path is what makes groups non-exchangeable.

Same men as Module 2. In this teaching example the stratum ratios are equal; in real data they differ somewhat, and we need one summary number.

One number for everyone: standardise

Ask: what would the risk be if all 100 men smoked, keeping the real mix of 40 drinkers and 60 non-drinkers?

If all smoked: 0.4 × 0.75 + 0.6 × 0.15 = 0.30 + 0.09 = 0.39

If none smoked: 0.4 × 0.25 + 0.6 × 0.05 = 0.10 + 0.03 = 0.13

Standardised risk ratio = 0.39 / 0.13 = 3.0

These are exactly the two futures of Module 1, rebuilt from data we could actually observe, once alcohol is accounted for.

Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 18, Stratification and Standardization.

Standardisation is a weighted average of the stratum risks, with the whole population's mix as weights. It answers the counterfactual question directly.

Adapted from Dr Ankur Joshi's lesson. Let students read the overall row first if you want the surprise.

Why: A was given the hard cases

  • 263 of A's 350 patients had large stones (75%).
  • 270 of B's 350 patients had small stones (77%).
  • Large stones are harder to treat, whatever the operation.

Stone size is a fork: it decided the operation and the outcome. The crude comparison mixes two different groups of patients.

Splitting by a variable is right only when the diagram says it is a common cause, as here. Splitting by a consequence of treatment can add bias.

Same logic as the alcohol strata earlier in this module, with real data.

A warning about the old rules

"Associated with both exposure and outcome"

A mediator and a collider (Modules 7 and 8) are associated with both too.

"It changed the estimate by 10%"

Adjusting for a mediator or a collider changes the estimate too, in the wrong direction.

Only the diagram can tell a confounder from the other two.

Hernán MA, Hernández-Díaz S, Werler MM, Mitchell AA. Am J Epidemiol 2002;155:176-84.

Plant the doubt now; Modules 7 and 8 deliver the counter-examples with numbers.

Module 7 · Causal inference for beginners

The chain: mediators

When the exposure works through a middle variable, adjusting for that variable removes the effect you wanted.

Module 7. When the exposure works through a middle variable, adjusting for that variable removes the effect you wanted.

The chain: a cause that works through something

Mothers who smoke in pregnancy have more infant deaths. How?

Smoking lowers birth weight.

Low birth weight raises the risk of infant death.

Low birth weight is a mediator: it lies on the causal path.

The chain: exposure, then mediator, then outcome. For now, suppose smoking acts only through birth weight.

Births where smoking acts only through birth weight

Made-up numbers, chosen to land near the real US figures you meet in Module 8.

The death risk depends only on birth weight, not on smoking directly. So all of smoking's effect runs through low birth weight.

The total effect of smoking

Smokers: 0.14 × 4% + 0.86 × 0.4% = 0.560% + 0.344% = 0.904%

Non-smokers: 0.05 × 4% + 0.95 × 0.4% = 0.200% + 0.380% = 0.580%

Risk ratio = 0.904 / 0.580 = 1.56

Smoking raises infant deaths by 56%. That is the total effect, the one a mother or a programme cares about.

904 against 580 per 100,000 births. Work it in the workbook first.

Now "adjust for birth weight"

SmokersNon-smokersRisk ratio
Low birth weight babies4%4%1.0
Normal weight babies0.4%0.4%1.0

Adjusted for birth weight, smoking looks harmless. Yet it raised deaths by 56%.

Stratifying on the mediator removes the very route the effect travels by. This is over-adjustment.

What happened, in the diagram

Green: a causal path, open. This carries the effect.

Adjusting for low birth weight blocks it (dashed). The effect is gone from the estimate, not from the babies.

Same blocking rule as Module 6: adjusting for a middle variable blocks the path. With a fork that is what we want; with a chain it is not.

How to spot a mediator

  • It is caused by the exposure.
  • It is usually measured after the exposure started.
  • Examples: cholesterol after starting hormones; CD4 count after starting ART; weight after joining a diet programme.

For the total effect, do not adjust, restrict, match or stratify on a mediator.

A rough but useful rule for beginners: be suspicious of any covariate measured after the exposure began.

No. Visits → Knowledge → Delivery is a chain: knowledge is a mediator. Adjusting would hide part of what the visits achieve.

What to carry forward

A chain carries the effect. The middle variable is a mediator.

Adjusting for a mediator removes part of the effect you want. In our numbers, 1.56 became 1.0.

The collider: selection bias

Two causes of the same thing become linked when you look only at people who have that thing.

Module 8. Two causes of the same thing become linked when you look only at people who have that thing.

The collider: two arrows that meet

Maternal smoking and birth defects: in the population, they are unrelated.

Smoking causes low birth weight.

Birth defects cause low birth weight too. The arrows collide.

A collider is a common effect. It is a property of a variable on a particular path: low birth weight is a collider on Smoking → LBW ← Defect.

The rule for colliders is the opposite

Left alone

A path through a collider is closed. No association flows. Smoking and defects stay unrelated.

Adjusted for, or selected on

The path opens. Smoking and defects become associated, though neither causes the other.

Why: among small babies, if the mother did not smoke, something else made the baby small. A defect becomes more likely.

This is the counter-intuitive one. Forks and chains are open until you adjust; colliders are closed until you adjust. Spend time on the intuition sentence.

A town where smoking and TB are unrelated

100,000 people. 30% smoke. 1% have TB. Smoking makes no difference to TB here.

TBNo TB
Smokers30029,700
Non-smokers70069,300

Odds ratio = (300 × 69,300) / (29,700 × 700) = 1.0

Made-up numbers. In reality smoking does raise TB risk; here we remove that to isolate the bias.

Start from no association at all, so that anything we find later is created by selection.

Admission is a common effect of smoking and TB: a collider.

Study only the admitted patients

Odds ratio = (270 × 1,386) / (1,485 × 560) = 0.45

In the ward, smoking seems to halve the odds of TB. In the town, it made no difference.

This is Berkson's bias, described in 1946 for hospital-based studies. Nothing about smoking or TB changed; only who we looked at.

Selection bias is adjusting for a collider

The path Smoking → Admitted ← TB is closed in the town.

Studying only admitted people conditions on admission: the path opens (red).

Selection bias: who entered or stayed in your study depends on both the exposure and the outcome.

Hernán MA, Hernández-Díaz S, Robins JM. Epidemiology 2004;15:615-25.

Same drawing convention as before: an adjusted box, here because of selection rather than analysis.

Reported rate ratio 1.55

Is smoking good for small babies?

Hernández-Díaz S, Schisterman EF, Hernán MA. Am J Epidemiol 2006;164:1115-20.

Real data. For decades this was called the birth weight paradox, and some argued smoking was protective for small babies. Let the room try to explain it before the next slide.

The paradox, drawn

Smoking lowers birth weight, which raises deaths.

Birth defects and other serious causes lower birth weight and raise deaths too. Not measured.

Smoking may also harm directly.

Restrict to low birth weight: a collider is adjusted for, and Smoking → LBW ← Defects → Death opens.

Low birth weight is both a mediator (Module 7) and a collider here. Restricting to it does both kinds of damage.

Module 10 · Causal inference for beginners

Choosing what to adjust for

Put the three building blocks together: a four-step recipe for picking an adjustment set from a diagram.

Module 10. Put the three building blocks together: a four-step recipe for picking an adjustment set from a diagram.

A recipe in four steps

  1. List every path between exposure and outcome.
  2. Keep the causal paths open: adjust for nothing on them.
  3. Block each back-door path by adjusting for a variable on it that is not a collider.
  4. Check that you have not opened anything: no colliders, nothing caused by the exposure.

This is Pearl's back-door criterion, in steps. Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 12.

Students now have every piece: forks to block, chains to leave alone, colliders to leave alone. The recipe just applies the three rules path by path.

The back-door rule, in two lines

1 · Block every back-door path

Every path that starts with an arrow into the exposure must pass through something you adjust for, and not only through colliders.

2 · Touch nothing downstream

Adjust for nothing the exposure causes: no mediators, nothing that follows from them.

If both hold, and the diagram is right, the adjusted estimate is the total effect.

"If the diagram is right" carries the weight. The rule moves the argument from statistics to subject knowledge.

Practice 1: tobacco chewing and oral cancer

Older men chew more and get more oral cancer.

Chewing causes leukoplakia, which can turn into cancer.

Chewers, and men with early symptoms, are more likely to attend a screening camp.

Practice diagram, drawn for teaching.

Build the diagram with the room. Then apply the four steps on the next slide.

Step 1: list the paths

Chewing → Leukoplakia → Cancer. Causal: keep open.

Chewing ← Age → Cancer. Back-door: open, must be blocked.

Chewing → Camp ← Cancer. Through a collider: already closed.

Green is causal and open; red is non-causal and open; dark is closed. Three paths, three different decisions.

Steps 2 to 4: decide

Leukoplakia: on the causal path. Do not adjust.

Camp attendance: a collider. Do not adjust, and do not study only camp attendees.

Age: blocks the only back-door path. Adjust.

Adjustment set: {Age}

Point out the design lesson: if the data came only from screening camp attendees, the study has already conditioned on the collider, and no analysis can undo it.

Your turn: Practice 2, diabetes and TB

Use the four steps. Which variables would you adjust for to estimate the total effect of diabetes on TB?

Is there more than one answer?

Give three minutes. Expect some to include HbA1c: it is caused by diabetes, so it fails rule 2.

Practice 2: the answer

Back-door 1: Diabetes ← Age → TB. Block with age.

Back-door 2: Diabetes ← Poverty → Undernutrition → TB. Block with poverty or undernutrition.

Two sets work: {Age, Poverty} or {Age, Undernutrition}. Never HbA1c.

Choose the set you can measure best.

Multiple valid sets is normal. Lab 2's "Show the sets that work" lists them for any diagram.

Practice 3: Tamil Nadu, again

Every set that works needs alcohol or peer group. Neither was measured.

With the data available, no adjustment set is enough.

Say so plainly. More adjustment on what you have cannot fix it.

Unmeasured confounding is the normal state of observational research. Module 12 shows what to do next: negative controls and a simple bias analysis.

What never to adjust for, and what is safe

VariableAdjust?Why
Common cause of exposure and outcomeYesBlocks a back-door path
Mediator, or anything caused by the exposureNoRemoves part of the effect
ColliderNoOpens a closed path
Cause of the outcome onlyOptionalNo bias; can narrow the confidence interval
Cause of the exposure onlyBetter notNo bias removed; widens the interval
The last row is the least intuitive. A pure cause of the exposure lies on no back-door path, so adjusting buys nothing and costs precision.

What to carry forward

List the paths. Keep causal ones open. Block back-doors with non-colliders. Open nothing new.

There may be several sets that work; use the best measured.

Sometimes none works with the data you have. Say so.

Module 14. Use everything so far on a published NFHS-4 analysis: draw the diagram, find the adjustment set, test the authors' model.

The study

Mondal and Paul used NFHS-4 (2015-16) to ask whether indoor air pollution raises acute respiratory infection (ARI) in children under five.

  • 247,743 children across India.
  • ARI: cough with short, rapid or difficult breathing in the last two weeks, as reported by the mother.
  • Main exposure: cooking with biomass fuel. "Biomass" included kerosene, coal, wood, crop waste and dung.
  • Result: adjusted odds ratio 1.10 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.20) for biomass fuel.

Mondal D, Paul P. PLoS ONE 2020;15(8):e0237611.

A real, recent, open-access Indian study. The aim is not to attack it but to practise the four steps on it.

Step 1: write the causal question

Not this

Is biomass fuel associated with ARI?

This

If households cooking with biomass switched to clean fuel, how much would ARI fall among their children under five?

Who, what change, what outcome. Now draw what else differs between biomass and clean-fuel households.

Module 1 again: a causal question names the change.

Step 2: start with eight boxes

Exposure and outcome first.

Where the household is: state, rural or urban.

Who the household is: wealth, mother's education.

The kitchen, and the child's age and sex.

The full lab has fifteen boxes. Starting with eight keeps the first pass manageable; Lab 4 lets groups add the rest.

Step 2, continued: draw the arrows you believe

Into fuel: LPG access depends on state and on rural or urban; richer and more educated households switch sooner.

Into ARI: place shapes outdoor air and care; educated mothers act on symptoms; the kitchen layout matters; young children get more ARI.

Between the others: state shapes urbanisation; place shapes wealth and schooling; wealth buys a separate kitchen.

Diagram drawn by the faculty for teaching; not the authors'. Yours may differ.

Pause after each group of arrows and ask whether anyone would leave one out. Leaving one out is a claim.

Step 3: find the back-door paths

Fuel ← Rural/urban → ARI

Fuel ← State → ARI

Fuel ← Education → ARI

Fuel ← Wealth → Kitchen → ARI

And several longer ones. Lab 4 lists them.

Four short back-door paths. Child age and sex lie on no back-door path: they cause ARI only.

Step 4: choose a set that blocks them

Under this diagram, two minimal sets work:

{State, Rural/urban, Wealth, Mother's education}

{State, Rural/urban, Mother's education, Separate kitchen}

Child age and sex are safe to add: they cause ARI only, so they can improve precision.

The second set works because kitchen blocks the wealth path and rural/urban blocks the route from place to wealth. Check it in Lab 4.

Now compare the paper's model

In the model

Separate kitchen, household smoking, child's age and sex, birth order, mother's age and education, caste, religion, wealth, TB in the household, crowding

Not in the model

Rural or urban residence. State.

Both are common causes of fuel and ARI in our diagram, so back-door paths stay open.

Under our diagram, the 1.10 is not protected from confounding by place.

Be fair: the authors had these variables in NFHS-4; a diagram drawn first would have flagged them.

Variable, same modelAdjusted ORReadable as its effect?
Biomass fuel1.10Only if the set suits fuel

Reading every row of a regression table as an effect is the "Table 2 fallacy". One model answers one causal question.

Westreich D, Greenland S. Am J Epidemiol 2013;177:292-8.

The authors called the education result surprising. Part of the answer: the model adjusted away education's effects through fuel and kitchen. Another part may be reporting: educated mothers may notice fast breathing more.

Two more things to ask any such study

Who reports the outcome?

Mothers report ARI. If education changes what they notice, education affects reported ARI, not only true ARI.

Is the exposure one thing?

Kerosene, coal, wood and dung were grouped as "biomass". A clean-fuel programme changes one of these, not all.

Measurement (Module 9) and well-defined exposure (Module 3), both developed further on the site.
