The HRT puzzle, once more

Nurses' Health Study

Women on oestrogen plus progestin: RR 0.39 for heart disease.

WHI trial

Women randomised to the same hormones: HR 1.29.

In Module 6 we blamed confounding by health-seeking. That is part of the story. This module adds another part: when follow-up started.

Grodstein et al. NEJM 1996; WHI JAMA 2002.

Who was in the cohort's "users"?

Most cohort users had been on hormones for years when follow-up began.

  • Women who had a heart attack soon after starting were already gone, or had stopped.
  • The users left were the survivors of the early risk.

The trial counted the early years. The cohort mostly missed them.

These are prevalent users. The bias is a form of selection, closer to Module 8 than Module 6.

Ask: what trial would you have run?

Before analysing observational data, write the protocol of the trial you wish you had. Then copy it with the data you have.

This is called emulating a target trial.

Hernán MA, Robins JM. Am J Epidemiol 2016;183:758-64.

The method forces explicit choices on eligibility, time zero and comparison.

Time zero, in one picture

In a trial, three things happen at the same moment:

  1. The person becomes eligible.
  2. The treatment is assigned.
  3. Follow-up starts.

When an observational analysis lets these drift apart, bias creeps in.

Prevalent users: follow-up starts long after treatment. Immortal time: treatment is assigned after follow-up starts.

Immortal time, in numbers

200 identical patients. A drug can only be started at month 6. It has no effect. Made-up numbers.

  • 20 die before month 6. They never had the chance to start.
  • Of the 180 alive, 90 start the drug and 90 do not. 9 die in each group.
Suissa S. Am J Epidemiol 2008;167:492-9.

Two ways to count

"Ever users" against "never users"

Users: 9 / 90 = 0.10

Never users: (20 + 9) / 110 = 0.26

RR = 0.38

Start the clock at month 6

Users: 9 / 90 = 0.10

Non-users: 9 / 90 = 0.10

RR = 1.0

Users had to survive six months to become users. That guaranteed survival is "immortal time".

The naive count makes a useless drug look 60% protective.

Your turn

A study compares mortality in TB patients who completed 6 months of treatment with those who did not, counting from diagnosis.

Where is time zero? What bias could make completion look more protective than it is?

Completers had to survive 6 months to complete: immortal time. Fix it by assigning at a common time zero, for example at diagnosis with the strategy "complete treatment", and following everyone from there.

Treatment completion itself is a mix of adherence and survival, also a well-defined exposure issue from Module 3.

What to carry forward

Write the trial you wish you had, then emulate it.

Line up eligibility, treatment and the start of follow-up at time zero.

Prevalent users and immortal time can make a drug look protective with no confounding at all: 0.38 against 1.0.
