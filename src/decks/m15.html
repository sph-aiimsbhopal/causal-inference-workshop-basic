Now your own question

You have the tools. This module turns them into a plan for your thesis or your next paper.

  1. Ask a causal question, out loud.
  2. Define the exposure as an intervention.
  3. Draw the diagram.
  4. Choose the adjustment set.
  5. Plan the analysis and the bias checks.
Students should bring a real question. The workbook page for Module 15 follows these five steps.

Step 1: say "cause"

Many papers say "associated with" but draw causal conclusions.

If you want to know whether changing X would change Y, say so. Then the methods can be judged against that aim.

Hernán MA. The C-word. Am J Public Health 2018;108:616-9.

Hiding the aim does not make the analysis less causal; it makes it harder to check.

A worked example: LPG and child ARI

Question. Would giving a household an LPG connection reduce ARI in its children under five?

Exposure. LPG as the main cooking fuel, against biomass, for at least a year. Module 3.

Who. Households in which both are possible: LPG is available and could be afforded. Positivity.

Outcome. ARI in the last two weeks, as reported by the mother. Note the measurement issue. Module 9.

Each line cites the module it draws on.

The written reason is the reviewer's checkpoint. If your diagram differs, your set will differ; that is fine if you show the diagram.

Step 5: the analysis plan, in five lines

  1. One model for the exposure, with the chosen set. Report its estimate only.
  2. State what population the estimate applies to.
  3. Name the main unmeasured confounder. Give the E-value.
  4. Say how the outcome was measured and which way the error may go.
  5. Attach the diagram as a figure.

Checklist before you submit

  • Is the exposure something you could imagine assigning?
  • Did you adjust for anything caused by the exposure?
  • Did selection into the study depend on exposure and outcome?
  • Does time zero line up eligibility, exposure and follow-up?
  • Do the results tables avoid causal labels for covariates?
