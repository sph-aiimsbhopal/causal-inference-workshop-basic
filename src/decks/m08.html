The collider: two arrows that meet

Maternal smoking and birth defects: in the population, they are unrelated.

Smoking causes low birth weight.

Birth defects cause low birth weight too. The arrows collide.

A collider is a common effect. It is a property of a variable on a particular path: low birth weight is a collider on Smoking → LBW ← Defect.

The rule for colliders is the opposite

Left alone

A path through a collider is closed. No association flows. Smoking and defects stay unrelated.

Adjusted for, or selected on

The path opens. Smoking and defects become associated, though neither causes the other.

Why: among small babies, if the mother did not smoke, something else made the baby small. A defect becomes more likely.

This is the counter-intuitive one. Forks and chains are open until you adjust; colliders are closed until you adjust. Spend time on the intuition sentence.

A town where smoking and TB are unrelated

100,000 people. 30% smoke. 1% have TB. Smoking makes no difference to TB here.

Odds ratio = (300 × 69,300) / (29,700 × 700) = 1.0

Made-up numbers. In reality smoking does raise TB risk; here we remove that to isolate the bias.

Start from no association at all, so that anything we find later is created by selection.

Admission is a common effect of smoking and TB: a collider.

Odds ratio = (270 × 1,386) / (1,485 × 560) = 0.45

In the ward, smoking seems to halve the odds of TB. In the town, it made no difference.

This is Berkson's bias, described in 1946 for hospital-based studies. Nothing about smoking or TB changed; only who we looked at.

Selection bias is adjusting for a collider

The path Smoking → Admitted ← TB is closed in the town.

Studying only admitted people conditions on admission: the path opens (red).

Selection bias: who entered or stayed in your study depends on both the exposure and the outcome.

Hernán MA, Hernández-Díaz S, Robins JM. Epidemiology 2004;15:615-25.

Same drawing convention as before: an adjusted box, here because of selection rather than analysis.

Where it hides in real studies

  • Hospital controls in a case-control study, as we just saw.
  • Loss to follow-up that depends on both exposure and illness.
  • Volunteers who join because they are exposed and worried.
  • Survivors. Women still taking hormones years later are those who did not have an early heart attack on them. Module 13 returns to this.

You cannot see this bias in the data. Only in how people got into the data.

The survivor point is the start of the full explanation of the hormone puzzle.

Is smoking good for small babies?

Hernández-Díaz S, Schisterman EF, Hernán MA. Am J Epidemiol 2006;164:1115-20.

Real data. For decades this was called the birth weight paradox, and some argued smoking was protective for small babies. Let the room try to explain it before the next slide.

The paradox, drawn

Smoking lowers birth weight, which raises deaths.

Birth defects and other serious causes lower birth weight and raise deaths too. Not measured.

Smoking may also harm directly.

Restrict to low birth weight: a collider is adjusted for, and Smoking → LBW ← Defects → Death opens.

Low birth weight is both a mediator (Module 7) and a collider here. Restricting to it does both kinds of damage.
