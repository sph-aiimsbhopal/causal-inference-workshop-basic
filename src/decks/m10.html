A recipe in four steps

  1. List every path between exposure and outcome.
  2. Keep the causal paths open: adjust for nothing on them.
  3. Block each back-door path by adjusting for a variable on it that is not a collider.
  4. Check that you have not opened anything: no colliders, nothing caused by the exposure.

This is Pearl's back-door criterion, in steps. Modern Epidemiology, 4th ed., chapter 12.

Students now have every piece: forks to block, chains to leave alone, colliders to leave alone. The recipe just applies the three rules path by path.

The back-door rule, in two lines

1 · Block every back-door path

Every path that starts with an arrow into the exposure must pass through something you adjust for, and not only through colliders.

2 · Touch nothing downstream

Adjust for nothing the exposure causes: no mediators, nothing that follows from them.

If both hold, and the diagram is right, the adjusted estimate is the total effect.

"If the diagram is right" carries the weight. The rule moves the argument from statistics to subject knowledge.

Practice 1: tobacco chewing and oral cancer

Older men chew more and get more oral cancer.

Chewing causes leukoplakia, which can turn into cancer.

Chewers, and men with early symptoms, are more likely to attend a screening camp.

Practice diagram, drawn for teaching.

Build the diagram with the room. Then apply the four steps on the next slide.

Step 1: list the paths

Chewing → Leukoplakia → Cancer. Causal: keep open.

Chewing ← Age → Cancer. Back-door: open, must be blocked.

Chewing → Camp ← Cancer. Through a collider: already closed.

Green is causal and open; red is non-causal and open; dark is closed. Three paths, three different decisions.

Steps 2 to 4: decide

Leukoplakia: on the causal path. Do not adjust.

Camp attendance: a collider. Do not adjust, and do not study only camp attendees.

Age: blocks the only back-door path. Adjust.

Adjustment set: {Age}

Point out the design lesson: if the data came only from screening camp attendees, the study has already conditioned on the collider, and no analysis can undo it.

Your turn: Practice 2, diabetes and TB

Use the four steps. Which variables would you adjust for to estimate the total effect of diabetes on TB?

Is there more than one answer?

Give three minutes. Expect some to include HbA1c: it is caused by diabetes, so it fails rule 2.

Practice 2: the answer

Back-door 1: Diabetes ← Age → TB. Block with age.

Back-door 2: Diabetes ← Poverty → Undernutrition → TB. Block with poverty or undernutrition.

Two sets work: {Age, Poverty} or {Age, Undernutrition}. Never HbA1c.

Choose the set you can measure best.

Multiple valid sets is normal. Lab 2's "Show the sets that work" lists them for any diagram.

Practice 3: Tamil Nadu, again

Every set that works needs alcohol or peer group. Neither was measured.

With the data available, no adjustment set is enough.

Say so plainly. More adjustment on what you have cannot fix it.

Unmeasured confounding is the normal state of observational research. Module 12 shows what to do next: negative controls and a simple bias analysis.

What never to adjust for, and what is safe

VariableAdjust?Why
Common cause of exposure and outcomeYesBlocks a back-door path
Mediator, or anything caused by the exposureNoRemoves part of the effect
ColliderNoOpens a closed path
Cause of the outcome onlyOptionalNo bias; can narrow the confidence interval
Cause of the exposure onlyBetter notNo bias removed; widens the interval
The last row is the least intuitive. A pure cause of the exposure lies on no back-door path, so adjusting buys nothing and costs precision.

Not sure about the whole diagram?

VanderWeele's practical rule needs only causes, not every arrow:

  1. Adjust for each variable that causes the exposure, the outcome, or both.
  2. Leave out any you believe causes the exposure only.
  3. Add a good proxy for an important unmeasured common cause.
  4. Never include anything caused by the exposure.

VanderWeele TJ. Principles of confounder selection. Eur J Epidemiol 2019;34:211-9.

Useful when a committee cannot agree on the full diagram. It avoids colliders and mediators as long as you know the time order.

What to carry forward

List the paths. Keep causal ones open. Block back-doors with non-colliders. Open nothing new.

There may be several sets that work; use the best measured.

Sometimes none works with the data you have. Say so.
