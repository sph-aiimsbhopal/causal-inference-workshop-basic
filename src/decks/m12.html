Some confounders were never measured

The Tamil Nadu study asked about smoking, age and education. It did not ask about alcohol.

If alcohol opens a back door we cannot close, is the 4.5 worthless? Not quite. We can ask how big the bias could be.

This is Module 2's cirrhosis clue, now made quantitative.

A clue in the data: a negative control

Smokers in the same study had 3.3 times the death rate from alcoholism or cirrhosis.

Smoking has little direct effect on deaths from alcoholism. So a large ratio here points to something smokers share: drinking.

A negative control is an outcome the exposure should not cause. If it is still associated, confounding is likely.

Lipsitch M, Tchetgen Tchetgen E, Cohen T. Epidemiology 2010;21:383-8.

Negative controls detect bias; they do not tell you its size by themselves.

Three numbers decide the bias

p1

Share of smokers who drink heavily.

p0

Share of non-smokers who drink heavily.

RRalcohol

How much heavy drinking raises the risk of TB death, at the same smoking status.

If smokers and non-smokers drink alike, or alcohol does nothing to TB, there is no bias.

Both arms of the fork must be present: alcohol linked to smoking (p1 differs from p0), and alcohol linked to the outcome (RR above 1).

The bias factor

Bias factor = [p1(RRalcohol − 1) + 1] ÷ [p0(RRalcohol − 1) + 1]

Corrected RR = observed RR ÷ bias factor

Read it as: how much more TB risk smokers carry from drinking alone.

This simple form assumes alcohol's effect is the same in smokers and non-smokers.

Rothman chapter 29 gives this formula. It is the external adjustment method.

Worked example: alcohol and the 4.5

Suppose 60% of smokers and 25% of non-smokers drink heavily, and drinking triples the risk of TB death. Assumed numbers.

Top: 0.60 × (3 − 1) + 1 = 2.2

Bottom: 0.25 × (3 − 1) + 1 = 1.5

Bias factor: 2.2 ÷ 1.5 = 1.47

Corrected: 4.5 ÷ 1.47 = 3.1

A strong confounder shrinks the estimate, but smoking still roughly triples the risk.

Students should do the arithmetic with you, line by line.

The E-value: one number for "how strong?"

The E-value is the strength a confounder would need, linked to both exposure and outcome, to explain away the whole estimate.

E-value = RR + √(RR × (RR − 1))

Smoking and TB death, RR 4.5:
4.5 + √(4.5 × 3.5) = 8.5

Biomass fuel and ARI, OR 1.10:
1.1 + √(1.1 × 0.1) = 1.4

VanderWeele TJ, Ding P. Ann Intern Med 2017;167:268-74.

For the fuel OR we treat the odds ratio as close to a risk ratio; ARI in two weeks is not rare, so this is rough.

Reading E-values

  • 8.5: a confounder would need to raise both smoking and TB death about eightfold. Few plausible ones are that strong.
  • 1.4: a modest confounder, such as household wealth measured crudely, could explain the biomass finding.
  • A large E-value does not prove causation. It only says how strong the hidden confounding would have to be.
E-values are easy to misuse as a pass mark. Pair them with a diagram and with what is known about the likely confounders.

What to carry forward

An unmeasured confounder is a question of size. Ask how strong it would need to be.

Bias factor from three numbers: p1, p0 and its effect on the outcome. Here 4.5 stayed above 3 in most worlds.

Small estimates like 1.10 are fragile: an E-value of 1.4.
